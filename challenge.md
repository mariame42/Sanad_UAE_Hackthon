 1  Challenge Statement                                                                              HMW


  How might we enable Student Accessibility Services (SAS) to identify students at risk of learning disabilities early within the university ecosystem to
   improve personalized support and referral outcomes under ethical AI and data privacy constraints using adaptive AI screening, analytics, and learning
    platform integration, while aligning with the UAE's digital transformation and inclusive education strategies?


 2  Problem Statement


   Some students experience persistent learning barriers without recognizing that support may be available or without feeling ready to disclose a concern. Relevant
    observations may arise through students, faculty, advisors, tutors, accessibility staff, and student-success services, but these signals are fragmented and can be
    interpreted inconsistently. Support may therefore begin only after repeated difficulty, avoidable stress, or a crisis referral.

   The required future state is an inclusive early-support model that offers timely assistance without labeling, monitoring, or penalizing students. It should distinguish
    general academic support from disability-related assessment, use meaningful consent and proportionate information, route concerns to qualified professionals, and
   ensure that every referral has clear ownership, human review, and an accessible path to support or recourse.


 3  Root Causes


    R1   The university lacks a shared distinction between learning difficulty, support need, screening concern, and formal disability determination: Different functions
        may interpret the same indicator differently, creating inconsistent outreach, referral thresholds, and expectations about who may assess or decide.


    R2    Support and referral pathways are fragmented across academic, accessibility, counseling, and student-success functions: Students and staff may not know
         where a concern should go, who owns the next step, what information is necessary, or how progress is followed across a handoff.


    R3   The operating model remains reactive and depends heavily on disclosure or visible academic decline: Students who compensate, avoid disclosure, or
          experience non-obvious barriers may remain outside formal support until challenges have intensified.


    R4    Frontline staff have uneven confidence, guidance, and safeguards for responding to early concerns: Faculty, advisors, tutors, and mentors may notice change
           but lack a consistent, non-diagnostic way to engage the student, offer support, and refer appropriately.


    R5    Referral, support, and outcome learning is not governed as a continuous improvement cycle: The institution may track contacts or accommodations without
           consistently examining referral appropriateness, timeliness, unmet demand, student trust, support effectiveness, and differential outcomes.

 4  Stakeholders — Power vs. Interest Matrix


     High Power / High Interest — Manage Closely

  ◆Student Accessibility Services and qualified accessibility professionals — Own accessibility intake, support planning, accommodation processes, and specialist referral
      boundaries.

  ◆Provost, Academic Affairs, and Student Affairs leadership — Set institutional responsibilities for learning quality, inclusion, student welfare, and academic integrity.

  ◆Student Success, Retention, and Academic Advising leadership — Coordinate general support, progression outreach, case management, and academic referrals.

   ◆Qualified assessment, counseling, and clinical referral professionals — Determine when specialist review is appropriate and protect professional assessment boundaries.

   ◆University data privacy, ethics, responsible-AI, and information-governance functions — Govern sensitive information, permissible use, safeguards, accountability, and
      assurance.



     High Power / Low Interest — Keep Satisfied

   ◆University executive leadership and governing bodies — Set institutional risk, inclusion, and student-success expectations without managing individual cases.

  ◆Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research and accreditation authorities — Establish quality and regulatory expectations while the university owns operational
       delivery

       delivery.

   ◆University legal, compliance, cybersecurity, and internal-audit functions — Provide oversight within broader institutional mandates.

   ◆University finance and human-resources functions — Influence specialist staffing and support capacity but do not determine individual support needs.



    Low Power / High Interest — Keep Informed

  ◆Students, including students who have not disclosed or requested support — Carry the direct benefits and risks of outreach, screening, referral, and data use.

  ◆Faculty members and teaching assistants — Observe learning participation and performance but should not diagnose or independently label students.

  ◆Academic advisors, tutors, learning-center staff, and peer mentors — Provide trusted first contact and can connect students to appropriate support.

  ◆Student representatives, disability advocates, and inclusive-education organizations — Contribute lived experience, accessibility expectations, and safeguards.

  ◆Parents, guardians, or sponsors where involvement is appropriate and authorized — May support the student but should not replace the student's agency or confidentiality.



    Low Power / Low Interest — Monitor

   ◆Accessibility and educational technology vendors — Supply tools but should not define risk, diagnosis, eligibility, or accommodation decisions.

  ◆External analytics consultants and general education researchers — May contribute methods without access to identifiable student cases or institutional decision rights.
